It all started with a conversation at the gym. I was talking to an experienced developer, and when he found out I wasn't using an AI-powered code editor, he was stunned. "You're not working with Cursor yet?" he asked. That question, combined with recommendations from my peers and a healthy dose of curiosity, was the push I needed. My main goal was simple: I wanted to work faster.

For those who haven't heard of it, Cursor is essentially a fork of Visual Studio Code that deeply integrates Large Language Models (LLMs). Its key differentiator is the ability to use your entire codebase as context, allowing the AI to assist with almost any question you can throw at it.

My first real test of its power was a shock to the system. I had a complex problem that I estimated would take at least half a day of manual coding. I carefully crafted a detailed prompt outlining the issue and what I needed. Ten minutes later, Cursor provided a complete, working solution. It felt like magic; that half-day task was done before my coffee got cold.

But as I continued to use it, I discovered the other side of the coin. Incredible speed comes with significant risk: the trap of "vibe coding." If you give Cursor too broad of a prompt, or if you aren't 100% certain what the solution should look like, the AI will gladly take the reins. It can rapidly fill your project with code that works on the surface, but that you don't understand at all. You're left with a functioning but alien codebase and no real knowledge of how it all fits together.

This experience led me to a powerful realization, an idea echoed by others in the community: instead of prompting an AI for a solution and then trying to understand its code, it's often a more effective and responsible process to write the code yourself and have the AI evaluate it.

Still, even with that caveat, my overall verdict is that Cursor is a win. It undeniably helps you work faster, especially when it comes to the mundane, boilerplate tasks every developer has to slog through. It will always give you a solution, and while it requires a critical human eye today, it's amazing to think how accurate these tools will become in the coming years.
